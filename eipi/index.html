Part one

1

Where counting starts, and why nothing is ever exactly one.

  1. counting

    Counting starts with one. One apple.

  2. No two apples are the same. Calling each one “one apple” ignores everything that makes it this apple.

  3. Wait.

    Is anything in the world exactly 1? Measure a one-metre rod closely enough and it’s always a little off.

  4. 1 is an idea we lay over things. That’s what makes it useful: every apple counts the same.

  5. ~300 BC

    Euclid wrote two definitions. A unit is what makes each thing “one”. A number is a multitude made of units.

  6. So a number meant many. 2 was the first number. One was what numbers were made of, not one of them.

    Chrysippus c. 280–207 BC

    A Stoic philosopher who argued that one is a number too. It took a long time for that to catch on.

  7. ~630

    Centuries later, Isidore of Seville still wrote that one is the seed of number, but not a number.

  8. 1585

    The Flemish engineer Simon Stevin said it flatly: the unit is a number.

    Simon Stevin 1548–1620

    He advised on windmills, locks and ports, tutored Prince Maurice of Nassau, and planned how to flood the lowlands in an invading army’s path by opening sluices. The same year he published a 29-page booklet that taught Europe decimal fractions, the 0.5 kind, written for surveyors, wine-gaugers and merchants.

  9. One is the idea we lay over things. No wonder it was the last to be seen as a number.

  10. Count by adding one at a time. +1, +1: two apples.

  11. Two more: four.

  12. ×1 is the move that changes nothing. Three apples, taken one time: still three.

  13. So that’s where 1 comes from: where counting starts, and the move that leaves everything as it was.

  14. Now we can

    • Count.
    • Treat different things as the same kind.
    • Leave something exactly as it is: ×1.
    ×1 do nothing

Part two

0

Nothing, and the long argument over whether it counts.

  1. Take two apples away from two.

  2. Wait.

    What’s left? Is nothing a number?

  3. ~300 BC

    Babylonian scribes wrote numbers in columns. An empty column was just a gap, until they began marking it with a sign. It held a place. It wasn’t yet a number.

  4. ~350

    Across the ocean, the Maya came up with a zero of their own, for their calendars.

  5. 628

    In India, Brahmagupta treated zero as a number with rules. A number minus itself is zero. Anything times zero is zero. He wrote rules for fortunes and debts too.

    Brahmagupta 598–c. 668

    He also tried dividing by zero, and said zero divided by zero is zero. Nobody has made that work since. The rest of his rules are the ones we still use.

  6. 1202

    Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, brought these numerals, zero included, to Europe in his Liber Abaci.

    Fibonacci c. 1170–c. 1250

    As a boy he went with his father, a merchant and customs official, to a trading post in Bugia, in what is now Algeria. That is where he first learned the numerals.

  7. 1299

    Not everyone trusted them. That year Florence’s bankers’ guild kept the new numerals out of its official records.

  8. +0 is the move that changes nothing.

  9. And zero is the centre of the line. Every stretch and turn to come happens around it.

  10. One move breaks: dividing by zero. How many nothings make 6? No amount does.

  11. So that’s where zero comes from: a gap that became a sign, then a number.

  12. Now we can

    • Write any number with ten digits, using empty places.
    • Say “none”, and calculate with it.
    • Leave a number as it is: +0.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing

Part three

i

A number that, times itself, makes −1. Getting there takes five steps.

I.

Negatives

Numbers counted things. What happens when you take away more than you have?

  1. Taking away makes sense now. Four take away two is two. Two take away two is zero.

  2. Wait.

    Take three apples from two?

  3. There’s nothing left to take. That’s impossible.

  4. But a trader can owe three apples, and a thermometer can read below zero. The ledger needs somewhere to put that.

  5. Unless numbers aren’t things. Put the apples on a line.

  6. Adding two is two steps right along the apple line.

  7. Taking three is three steps left. Nothing stops you at zero. You land on −1.

  8. Negative numbers build the other side of the line. Three from two is possible now: −1.

  9. So a number is a move. +2 is two steps right, wherever you start.

  10. −3 is three steps left.

  11. So that’s where negative numbers come from.

  12. 1544

    Plenty of people hated this. Michael Stifel called them absurd numbers.

    Michael Stifel c. 1487–1567

    An Augustinian monk who took Luther’s side in the Reformation, a split in the church, not in mathematics. A pro-Luther poem in 1522 made things tense enough that he left his monastery. He also did “word calculations” on the letters of the Bible, and in 1532 announced that the world would end at 8 a.m. on 19 October 1533. It didn’t, and he made no more predictions.

  13. 1545

    Gerolamo Cardano used them in his great book of algebra, and called them fictitious.

    Gerolamo Cardano 1501–1576

    Born illegitimate in Pavia, which got him turned away by Milan’s college of physicians. He became a famous doctor anyway.

    He was also a compulsive gambler. At one low point he pawned his wife’s jewellery and the family landed in the poorhouse. Out of the dice came a book on games of chance, the first real study of probability, written around 1564 and not printed until 1663.

    In 1539 Niccolò Tartaglia gave him the solution of the cubic, in verse, after Cardano swore not to publish it. Then Cardano found that Scipione del Ferro had solved it first, and printed it in his Ars Magna in 1545, with credit to both. Tartaglia fought him for a decade.

    In 1560 his eldest son was beheaded for poisoning his wife. In 1570 the Inquisition jailed him for heresy. One charge: he had cast a horoscope of Jesus.

    He wrote his autobiography, De Vita Propria, in Rome. Legend says he predicted the day he would die, and some say he made sure of it. He died there in 1576.

  14. Now a new kind of move. Not a step, but a stretch.

  15. Multiplying is repeated groups. Three bags of two apples: six.

  16. On the apple line, ×3 sends 2 to 6, and 1 to 3. Everything moves three times as far from zero. Zero stays put.

  17. Add 2 to every point. 0 goes to 2, 1 to 3, 2 to 4. Every point moves the same distance.

  18. Now multiply every point by 2. 1 goes to 2, 2 to 4, 3 to 6. Far points move further. 0 doesn’t move at all.

  19. Owe 2 apples, three times over, and you owe 6. (−2) × 3 = −6. The stretch works on the other side too.

  20. And ×−1? It turns 2 into −2, a credit into a debt. Watch the dots.

  21. Every point swings over the top, round zero, to its mirror side. ×−1 is a half turn: 180°.

  22. Do it again and they keep going, round the bottom, back home. ×−1 ×−1 → ×1. That’s why (−5) × (−5) = 25.

  23. 1758

    Francis Maseres called that nonsense, and argued negatives should be cut out of algebra. Two half turns answer him.

    Francis Maseres 1731–1824

    A London lawyer and judge, once attorney general of Quebec, still in the wig and ruffles of his youth long after they went out. He paid to reprint old mathematics out of his own pocket, and slowed algebra teaching in Britain for decades.

  24. So a multiplication is a stretch, maybe with a flip. Stack a few.

    2

  25. What numbers were

    Amounts. How many apples. Less than none made no sense.

    What numbers are

    Moves on a line. Adding is a step. Multiplying is a stretch. ×−1 is a half turn around zero.

    Now we can

    • Owe things: debts, temperatures below zero, steps behind the start.
    • Solve x + 3 = 1.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing+ step× stretch×−1 flip

    Each new kind of number will add a move the old ones couldn’t make.

II.

Between

The line only stops at whole numbers. What lives in between?

  1. Wait.

    The apple line only stops at whole numbers. What’s between 0 and 1?

  2. Half an apple has nowhere to go.

  3. Cut an apple down the middle. Each half is ½. Now there’s a stop between 0 and 1.

  4. Halving is a move too: ×½ squeezes the whole apple line toward zero. Twice makes quarter apples.

  5. Cut apples into thirds, fifths, sevenths: new stops between the old ones. Fractions describe the in-between, which whole numbers couldn’t.

  6. ~1550 BC

    Egyptian scribes used fractions all the time. The Rhind papyrus is full of them.

    Ahmes scribe, c. 1550 BC

    He copied the papyrus from an older text that is now lost. It writes fractions as sums of pieces of one, like 2/5 = 1/3 + 1/15, with a table of them for every odd number up to 101.

  7. ~375 BC

    Greek mathematicians didn’t count them as numbers. A number was a count of whole units, and the one couldn’t be split. In Plato’s Republic, the experts mock anyone who tries.

  8. ½, 0.5, 2/4, 50%: many names, one point.

  9. 0.5 is a fraction too: 5/10. Decimals are fractions with 10, 100, 1000 on the bottom.

  10. Some names never finish. ⅓ is 0.333…, with 3s running on forever, for the same point. The number is the point. The symbols are names for it.

  11. So that’s where fractions come from. The line now has a stop wherever you can cut.

  12. Now we can

    • Share evenly: half an apple, a third of a day.
    • Measure parts of a unit.
    • Solve 2x = 1.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing+ step× stretch×−1 flip×½ cut

III.

Gaps within gaps

Fractions seem to fill the line. They don’t quite.

  1. Fractions seem to fill everything. Surely that’s every number.

  2. Multiply a number by itself and you get a square. Sides of 3 hold 9 little squares: 3 × 3 = 9, written 3².

  3. It’s the stretch move, used on itself.

  4. Run it backwards. Which side gives an area of 4? 2. An area of 9? 3.

  5. An area of 2? Which x gives x² = 2?

  6. Draw a square with sides of 1, then the square on its diagonal. It’s made of four half-squares: area exactly 2.

    Plato’s Meno

    Socrates draws this in the sand for a boy who has never studied geometry and, only asking questions, leads him to it: the square twice as big is the square on the diagonal.

  7. So the diagonal is the side whose square is 2.

  8. Builders and surveyors walk that diagonal across square plots. Its length has to be a number.

  9. ~1700 BC

    Babylonian scribes measured it closely. A clay tablet, YBC 7289, gives it as about 1.41421296.

  10. Close, but would it ever come out exact?

  11. ~500 BC

    Greek mathematicians asked which fraction it is. Swing the diagonal down onto the line.

  12. Wait.

    Zoom in where it lands. It’s between 7/5 and 3/2.

  13. Zoom in as far as you like. Two fractions on either side, and √2 always in the space between.

  14. No fraction ever lands on it. You can walk that length, yet as a fraction it’s impossible. There’s a hole in the line.

  15. √2 fills the hole. So that’s where irrational numbers come from. They add no new move. They fill the holes.

    Hippasus 5th century BC

    Writers centuries later tell of a Pythagorean drowned at sea for giving away a secret. In some versions it was the irrational, in others a twelve-sided solid. The accounts disagree, and none is close to the time. Almost certainly a story.

  16. Now we can

    • Measure any diagonal, any length you can draw.
    • Solve x² = 2.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing+ step× stretch×−1 flip×½ cut√ fill

IV.

A quarter turn

Every number times itself lands on the positive side. What could land on −1?

  1. Squaring a number is doing its move twice. ×3, then ×3 again: ×9. So 3² = 9.

  2. ½ × ½ = ¼. Shrink, then shrink again.

  3. (−2) × (−2) = 4. Stretch and flip, then stretch and flip again. The flips cancel.

  4. (−1) × (−1) = 1. Two half turns: a full circle.

  5. 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2: the move done three times. The little number counts how many times.

  6. Every square lands on the positive side, whichever side it starts from.

  7. Wait.

    What move, done twice, lands on −1?

  8. Nothing on the line. Plus times plus is plus, minus times minus is plus. It’s impossible.

  9. 1545

    Cardano had already run into √−15 in a problem. He called the arithmetic as subtle as it is useless.

  10. Why bother? Take x³ = 15x + 4. The answer is plainly 4.

  11. But the cubic formula only reaches 4 by going through √−121. Its path leaves the line and comes back.

  12. 1572

    Rafael Bombelli took that detour seriously, and wrote down rules for calculating with it.

    Rafael Bombelli 1526–1572

    A wool merchant’s son from Bologna who never went to university. He became a hydraulic engineer, draining marshes in the Val di Chiana, and started his Algebra while that project was on hold. Three books came out in 1572, the year he died. The last two were never finished.

  13. Remember, ×−1 is a half turn. So we need a move that, done twice, makes a half turn.

  14. So what is half of a half turn?

  15. Nowhere on the line is halfway round. The line isn’t enough.

  16. So the dot lifts off it. The line becomes a plane, and every point has two parts: across and up.

  17. Call that move ×i. Two quarter turns make a half turn.

  18. So i × i = −1. The impossible question has an answer: a quarter turn.

  19. So that’s where imaginary numbers come from.

  20. 1637

    René Descartes named them imaginary. He meant it as an insult.

  21. 1799

    A surveyor, Caspar Wessel, drew numbers as points on a plane.

    Caspar Wessel 1745–1818

    Norwegian, one of fourteen children of a minister, and poor most of his life. He spent decades triangulating Denmark and drew its first accurate map. This was his only mathematics paper. He wasn’t an Academy member, so a colleague read it for him in 1797. Almost no one noticed it until 1895.

  22. 1806

    In Paris, Jean-Robert Argand had the same idea, and left his name off it.

    Jean-Robert Argand 1768–1822

    He had a few copies printed and sent one to Legendre, who was unimpressed and mentioned it in a letter. In 1813 Jacques Français found that letter among his late brother’s papers, published the idea, and asked the unknown author to come forward. Argand did. Little else about him is certain. The usual story that he kept books in Paris may be about another man with the same name.

  23. Gauss later suggested calling them lateral numbers. Sideways ones.

  24. Try ×i on an ordinary number. 2 turns a quarter, to 2i.

  25. ×2 then ×i: stretch, then turn. Together they make one move, ×2i.

  26. So every point in the plane is a move. Which move? Look at where it sends 1.

  27. Call that point b. The triangle from 0 to 1 to b is the move.

  28. To multiply a by b, do the same move to a: the same triangle, scaled and turned to sit on a.

  29. Same shape, so lengths multiply and angles add.

  30. Try it. Drag a. Pick a move, and tap it again to repeat it.

  31. Graph paper has points but no way to multiply them. This plane does.

    William Rowan Hamilton 1843

    Hamilton spent years trying to do the same in three dimensions. In October 1843 his children asked him every morning whether he could multiply triplets yet. He could only add them. On the 16th, walking along a Dublin canal with his wife, he saw how to do it in four, and cut the rule into Broom Bridge with a knife: i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1.

  32. Anything that turns gets computed this way: alternating current, radio waves, a sprite rotating on screen.

  33. Now we can

    • Treat a turn as a number.
    • Solve x² = −1, and finish Bombelli’s cubic.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing+ step× stretch×−1 flip×½ cut√ fillπ beyond roots×i turn

V.

The ladder

Each new kind of number answered an impossible equation. Does that ever stop?

  1. x + 3 = 1 was impossible. Negatives made it possible: −2.

  2. 2x = 1 was impossible in whole numbers. Fractions: ½.

  3. x² = 2 was impossible in fractions. √2.

  4. x² = −1 was impossible on the line. i.

  5. Each rung is a short recipe of multiplying and adding x. Mathematicians call these polynomials.

  6. Try harder recipes. Every answer is already in the plane.

  7. 1799

    Carl Friedrich Gauss, at 22, argued that every polynomial has its answers in the plane. The ladder stops here.

    Carl Friedrich Gauss 1777–1855

    It was his doctoral thesis. The proof had a gap nobody closed until 1920. He kept coming back to the theorem and published his last proof of it in 1849, fifty years after the first.

  8. With i, no equation like these is impossible any more. Every one has its answers here.

  9. The numbers are complete. You can stop inventing new ones.

  10. Here are the answers of every polynomial whose numbers are all 1 or −1, thousands of them. Raise the highest power.

  11. xn = 1 has n answers, spaced evenly round the circle.

  12. Now we can

    • Solve every polynomial equation. No impossible ones are left.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing+ step× stretch×−1 flip×½ cut√ fillπ beyond roots×i turn✓ complete

Part four

π

Fractions and roots seem to fill the line. Most of it is something else.

  1. Every number so far answers a polynomial. −2 answers x + 3 = 1. ½ answers 2x = 1. √2 answers x² = 2. i answers x² = −1.

  2. Scatter the answers of lots of polynomials on the line. They seem to be everywhere.

  3. Wait.

    Is every number the answer to some polynomial?

  4. Here’s a suspect. Roll a circle of width 1 along the line. One full turn covers a distance called π, about 3.14.

  5. Since ancient Greece, people tried to draw a square with exactly the area of a circle, using only ruler and compass. For over two thousand years, nobody managed it, and nobody could prove it impossible.

  6. 1844

    Joseph Liouville built a number that answers no polynomial at all, by hand, with 1s further and further apart. Numbers like this are called transcendental.

  7. 1882

    Ferdinand von Lindemann proved π is one. That settled it: the circle can’t be squared.

  8. So that’s where transcendental numbers come from: numbers no polynomial can reach.

  9. Now throw darts at the line.

  10. Zoom in on where one lands. The fractions and polynomial answers thin out to nothing around it.

  11. Almost every number on the line is transcendental. We can name only a handful.

  12. Now we can

    • Name numbers that no polynomial can reach.
    • Close the two-thousand-year question of squaring the circle.
    • Say where a dart thrown at the line almost surely lands.
    ×1 do nothing+0 nothing+ step× stretch×−1 flip×½ cut√ fillπ beyond roots

Part five

e

Growth that never stops, and the turn that ties everything together.

I.

Forever

Recipes that never end, and what they make.

  1. What if the recipe never ends?

  2. Money earning interest grows out of itself. So does a population. A cooling cup of coffee shrinks the same way.

  3. Grow 1 by 100% in one go: 2.

  4. Grow in two halves instead, 50% and then 50% of that: 2.25. A bit more.

  5. Cut it into more and more steps. The total climbs, then stalls near 2.718. That number is e.

  6. Keep growing like that for a time x and you reach ex. Its speed at each moment, the arrow, equals its size.

  7. A second road to e, also without end: 1 + 1 + ½ + ⅙ + 1/24 + … Each step divides by one more.

  8. 1873

    e turns out to be transcendental too, like π. Charles Hermite proved it.

  9. Nobody knows whether π + e is.

  10. For ex it’s 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 + … Work out how fast each piece grows and it slides down a slot. Same series: it is its own speed.

  11. Remember, 2² is the move ×2 done twice.

  12. 2½ is half that move: a move that, done twice, makes ×2. So 2½ = √2.

  13. 2√2 gets closed in on the same way: 21.4, 21.41, 21.414, …

  14. 1934

    Aleksandr Gelfond and Theodor Schneider: 2√2 is transcendental too.

    David Hilbert 1919

    Hilbert put this question on his famous list of problems in 1900. In a 1919 lecture he reportedly told the audience that no one in the hall would live to see it answered. It took fifteen years.

  15. Coastlines and ferns repeat their shape at every size. No short recipe draws them.

  16. Another kind of forever: repeat. z → z² + c, again and again. Some points run away. Some never do.

  17. Mark every c that never runs away.

  18. And ex = 0? Halve, halve, halve. Never zero. That one is off the ladder.

  19. Now we can

    • Describe growth that feeds on itself: interest, populations, cooling.
    • Add up recipes that never end.
    • Draw shapes that repeat forever, like the Mandelbrot set.

II.

Growth turns

What happens when growth gets pushed sideways.

  1. Back to ex, pushed by its own size. The bigger it gets, the harder the push.

  2. Put i in the exponent, eix, and the push is turned a quarter, ×i. It always points sideways.

  3. Pushed sideways forever, you go round in a circle.

  4. Watch the circling dot’s shadows. Across is called cos θ, up is sin θ, where θ is how far round it has gone.

  5. Build it from small steps. Start at 1. Push sideways by iπ/n of where you are, n times.

  6. Smaller steps bend onto the circle and land halfway round, at −1.

  7. 1748

    So eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ: go θ round the circle. Leonhard Euler wrote it down.

    Leonhard Euler 1707–1783

    He lost the sight of one eye around 1738 and was almost completely blind from 1766. His output went up. With scribes and a huge memory (he could recite the whole Aeneid), he averaged about a paper a week in 1775. He said some of his best ideas came with a baby in his arms and children playing at his feet.

  8. Chain a few spinning arrows of different sizes and speeds, and the tip draws a wave. Any sound can be pulled apart into arrows like these and rebuilt.

  9. Now we can

    • Write turning with e: eiθ.
    • Build waves and sounds out of spinning arrows.

III.

Sequency and simultaneity

An arrow, a circle, and a novel about time.

  1. 1974

    e to a real power: an arrow. Time as a line. Sequency.

  2. e to an imaginary power: a circle. Time as return. Simultaneity.

  3. In Ursula K. Le Guin’s The Dispossessed, the physicist Shevek spends the book trying to hold both in one theory.

    Ursula K. Le Guin 1929–2018

    Born in Berkeley. Her father, Alfred Kroeber, was an anthropologist. Her mother, Theodora Kroeber, wrote Ishi in Two Worlds. The Dispossessed won both the Hugo and the Nebula.

  4. Growth and turning at once: a spiral.

  5. A rhyme, not physics.

  6. Halfway round the circle from 1 is −1.
