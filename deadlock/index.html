Mechanics

How a match works: streets, soldiers, souls, heroes. And a robot fighting for his mother.

The game in one breath

Two gods, two teams, three streets

Half strategy game, half shooter. Drag the slider to see the difference.

From above, it's a MOBA: you command one hero like a piece on a board. Behind the shoulder, it's a shooter: you aim with your own hand. Deadlock keeps the board and hands you the gun.

You can't buy it. It's a free, invite-only playtest: a friend has to let you in. In October 2026, 303,347 people were playing at once anyway.

The map

Three streets, three tugs of war

Every 30 seconds, both bases send soldiers down every street. Left alone, they cancel out forever.

Try No heroes, then Archmother abandons York, at high speed. The lane they leave collapses; the lanes they reinforce push back. Whole matches are made of that trade. Until February 2025 there were four streets; Valve cut one.

The economy

Souls: money you earn by shooting light

One currency, souls: it is your money and your level at once.

Try it. Green is yours, orange is theirs, and the enemy down the street is shooting too.

Sharing is generous: two heroes beside a dying trooper each get 54%, not 50%. And the game leans against runaway leads. Defeating someone much richer than their teammates pays a big bonus, and teams that fall behind earn more from everything.

Who you play, and what you buy

Forty-four heroes, a shop, and a hundred ways to build

Twelve players, 44 heroes to choose from. Hover a card to meet them.

Each hero has a gun, a punch, and four powers. The fourth, the ultimate, unlocks last and changes fights.

There are no fixed jobs like tank or healer. Valve gives each hero three words instead, and players invent the rest.

The shop

Unspent souls buy items, in three colors.

Orange

Weapon

Your gun: more damage, faster fire, bigger magazines, special effects when bullets land.

Green

Vitality

Staying alive: health, healing, resistance to damage, speed.

Purple

Spirit

Your abilities: stronger effects, shorter waits between uses, longer reach.

Same hero, three different players. Buy items and watch the point move.

Prices and slots are the game's. Most item names are stand-ins for whole tiers.

A hidden bonus rewards committing to one color, so builds tend to pick a lane: gun, body, or magic.

A story · Heroes

A robot fighting for his mother

In a New York junkyard, a widow named Shelly built herself a son out of scrap and named him after the jazz they played at night. Now she is sick. Bebop fights in Deadlock for money to pay for her medicine, and he hasn't told her.

A story · October 2026

22,194 people who believed in a nurse

Players voted on which new hero Valve would release next. Over 110 million votes later, Nurse Harrow came last. When she finally arrived, Valve's patch notes thanked the people who had voted only for her.

Getting around

Movement: a city you can climb

Classic strategy maps are flat. The Cursed Apple has roofs, fire escapes, sewers and subways, and every hero can climb all of it.

The fancy moves spend stamina: usually three bars, one refilling every few seconds.

Get to the water tower. Stuck? Press Watch a route.

Ziplines: the lanes are also railways

A glowing rail runs above each lane, in its color. Hook on and it carries you out to the fight.

It only reaches as far as your soldiers have pushed. Winning a lane makes you faster.

Get shot while riding and you fall, stunned.

Hero powers

Moves of their own

Many heroes bend these rules. Lash swings on a grapple, Ivy carries a teammate through the air, Mirage teleports across the city, and Mo & Krill burrow underground.

Up and down

Fights on many floors

A fight can start on a rooftop and end in a sewer. That's the shooter half of Deadlock.

Thirty-five minutes

A match, minute by minute

The city wakes up on a schedule. Every few minutes, something new appears worth fighting over.

Drag the timeline or press play. Unlock times are the game's; which towers fall when is one made-up match.

The towers lose their armor on a clock, and soldiers come faster as time goes on, so every match has to end.

Every prize between the lanes pulls heroes away from their soldiers. That trade is most of what players argue about.

First ten minutes: two-on-two duels on single streets. Last ten: twelve people in one fight that decides everything.

Teamplay & competition

Playing together, from 1978 terminal rooms to a team that lost on purpose.

Playing together

Fifty years of finding each other

Every time computers learned to talk to each other, people used it to play together, usually within a year.

First we typed. Then we talked. Now the voice channel is the clubhouse.

Sociologists call places like cafés and barbershops third places: not home, not work, somewhere you see the regulars. A 2006 study argued online games had become one. For a lot of players a Deadlock evening is closer to pickup basketball at the same court every night than to a board game.

Bigger was never the point. Team games stayed the size of a team.

You couldn't buy Deadlock. A friend had to let you in.

Valve shipped no ads. It grew through Steam friends lists, Discord servers and streamers, the same way Gmail's invites spread in 2004. Then players got a say: in October 2026 they voted in every pre-match lobby on which new hero would arrive next.

The same closeness has a cost. Long team games under pressure produce a lot of blame and abuse in chat, and every online game, Deadlock included, has to keep building ways to report and mute it.

Teams, trophies, heartbreak

Competition

Five friends in a glass booth, a stadium holding its breath.

Valve's Dota 2 world championship, The International, began in 2011 with a prize that changed esports: $1 million to the winning team. Valve then let fans fund the pool by buying in-game items. It grew to $40 million.

Then, in 2023, Valve stopped the fan funding. The pool fell from tens of millions to about three.

Valve also ended its official league that year. Some teams lost salaries; independent organizers filled the calendar. Players love Valve's hands-off style, and it is also why their jobs feel fragile.

The best story in esports is about friends, not talent.

Valve's 2014 documentary Free to Play follows three players to that first International: Dendi from Ukraine, hyhy from Singapore, Fear from the US. It is mostly about families who doubted a game could be a life. Fear, the oldest, finally won in 2015.

Deadlock's scene

So far, it belongs to the players. Valve has run no Deadlock tournaments. Fans organize weekly brackets in custom lobbies, and every prize ever awarded adds up to $57,610. A real ranked mode arrived on 30 July 2026: sixty wins just to enter. Valve's patch note warned:

"We expect the next week or so to be very rocky."Deadlock update, 30 July 2026

A story · May 2026 community final

The team that lost on purpose

Melee Creeps bought eggs that grow in value, then let the other team beat them for fifteen minutes. Losing charged the game's comeback prize. At minute fifteen they cracked the eggs, took the prize, and the other team walked out. Valve patched the trick that same day.

Deathy's first LAN

A Montreal streamer who co-founded Death Slam, which ran Deadlock's first in-person tournament. Before Valve built any stage, players with day jobs built their own.

Architecture

The Cursed Apple, and the real New York underneath it.

The setting

The Cursed Apple

Every match happens in one city: the Cursed Apple. New York, with a hex on it.

The towers are 1899 to 1932, the clothes look 1930s, the neon looks 1950s. It's a collage of decades, all of them haunted.

From a street grid to three lanes

1811: planners laid a grid over Manhattan. Broadway was older and ran diagonally through it anyway.

Deadlock needed three long roads between two homes. It borrowed three real street names: York, Broadway, Greenwich.

Why the towers are shaped like wedding cakes

Those stepped towers aren't a style. They're a law: New York's 1916 zoning rule, written so streets would still get sunlight.

Hugh Ferriss drew what the law allowed, in four famous charcoal stages. His dark drawings are the ancestor of every noir New York, this one included.

The skyline is a real race

In 2024 a player named silvershroud matched 30 of Deadlock's skyline buildings to real ones, photo beside screenshot. It is one of the best things the community has made.

In date order, they replay the race for the tallest building in the world.

Trains in the sky

From 1868 to the 1950s, New Yorkers rode trains above the avenues. In Deadlock, heroes still do.

Mods & movies

Fans who rebuilt games, and made films out of them.

Made by players

Mods & movies

The best ideas in this genre were gifts. Fans took games apart, made something new, and gave it away.

A mod is a change players make to a game they didn't create: a new skin, a new map, sometimes a whole new set of rules. Make one below. Drag a tower, bend a street, recolor the soldiers. Then pass it on.

Some gifts grew into whole genres. Valve hired the people who made several of them.

Deadlock has no official mod tools yet. Players make skins and sound swaps anyway, trading them on sites like GameBanana; only the person who installs a mod sees it. Valve has said hero cosmetics are planned.

Movies made from games

Valve made Team Fortress 2's famous Meet the Team shorts with an in-house tool. In 2012 it gave that tool away.

It was called Source Filmmaker, and it turned players into directors. The same characters, lights and cameras Valve's animators used were suddenly free. A yearly contest, the Saxxy Awards, flew winners to Valve. Valve's own 15-minute TF2 short Expiration Date (2014) answered back. In 2023 a fan studio released Emesis Blue, a 108-minute horror film made entirely inside it, over about four years.

Fan film is older than the tool: Quake players told a story with recorded gameplay in Diary of a Camper (1996), and Halo's Red vs. Blue (2003) ran for twenty years. Deadlock runs on the same engine family, and its heroes are already turning up in fans' animation projects.

History

Where it comes from

Three families, one game

Deadlock has three parents: strategy games, team shooters and first-person shooters. Many of their ancestors were made for free, by fans.

Hollow rings are fan-made. Brass dots mark people Valve hired. Ticks are games that shut down; many tried to merge these families before Deadlock, and most of them ended there.

An invite-only playtest, never sold, and more people at once than Team Fortress 2 ever had.

The makers

Who is Valve, and why do people trust it?

A few hundred people near Seattle who make games and run Steam, the biggest PC game store. Steam pays the bills, so their games never have to rush.

Three habits run through everything they make. Try each one.

Habit one: make things readable at a glance

In a team fight you have a split second to know who's who. Valve's answer, from Team Fortress 2 (2007): shape first, then color, then detail.

Answer a few questions with the slider on the left, where everyone is the same soldier. Then drag it right.

Habit two: design feelings over time

Left 4 Dead (2008) had an AI Director that watched how stressed players were and paced the zombies: build up, peak, breathe.

Compare it with a spawner that sends enemies at random.

Habit three: teach by letting people discover

Valve rarely explains with text. Portal teaches one idea per room and lets you find it.

Three rooms, no words. Reach the door on the right.

The habit behind the habits: watch people play

Behind all three: watching strangers play, silently, over and over.

"Game designs are hypotheses. Playtests are experiments."Mike Ambinder, Valve, GDC 2009

How Deadlock carries all of this

  • The playtest is the game. Deadlock has been "invite-only" since 2024. You get in when a friend invites you. Even so, it hit 303,347 players at once in October 2026, more than Team Fortress 2's all-time record, without ever going on sale.
  • Unfinished on purpose. New heroes sometimes arrive in an experimental mode with placeholder art, so Valve can test how they play before paying for how they look. Players have even voted on which heroes ship next.
  • Constant tuning. Patch notes are long, dry lists of small number changes across many heroes at once, in the style IceFrog used for Dota. In 2025 Valve switched from updates every two weeks to fewer, larger ones.
  • "Valve time." Development reportedly began around 2018 under the codename Citadel. Valve still calls the game unfinished.

It doesn't always work. Artifact (2018) skipped the long public testing and collapsed in months. Deadlock is the opposite experiment.

Pizza at midnight

Fans noticed pizza shops near Valve getting busy just before big updates. Valve played along: the night before one, a developer posted a table full of pizza.
