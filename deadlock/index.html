Night over the Cursed Apple

An explorable guide for people who don't play gamesDeadlock

Twelve people, two gods, three streets, one haunted New York. This is how Valve's strangest game works, where it came from, and why it is built the way it is. Everything with a frame around it can be played with.

I
The game in one breath

Two gods, two teams, three streets

Half strategy game, half shooter. Drag the slider to see the difference.

From above, it's a MOBA: you command one hero like a piece on a board. Behind the shoulder, it's a shooter: you aim with your own hand. Deadlock keeps the board and hands you the gun.

You can't buy it. It's a free, invite-only playtest: a friend has to let you in. In October 2026, 303,347 people were playing at once anyway.

II
The map

Three streets, three tugs of war

Every 30 seconds, both bases send soldiers down every street. Left alone, they cancel out forever.

Try No heroes, then Archmother abandons York, at high speed. The lane they leave collapses; the lanes they reinforce push back. Whole matches are made of that trade. Until February 2025 there were four streets; Valve cut one.

III
The economy

Souls: money you earn by shooting light

Deadlock has one currency, souls, and it does two jobs at once. Every soul you earn makes your hero permanently stronger, and the souls you haven't spent yet buy items in shops. Whoever collects more souls usually wins.

You get souls from almost everything: killing troopers, killing enemy heroes, clearing the monsters that live between the lanes, smashing crates, knocking down towers. Even cockroaches in the subway tunnels are worth one soul each. But the steadiest income, especially early, is the troopers in your lane. Each one is worth about 100 souls, a little more every minute.

The orb

Here is Deadlock's signature twist. In older games of this kind you got paid only if your attack landed the killing blow on a soldier, the last hit. In Deadlock, when a trooper dies its bounty splits in two. Half drops to the ground for the team that killed it. The other half pops out of the body as a glowing soul orb that floats up for about a second.

Anyone can shoot that orb. Shoot your own team's orb and you confirm it, locking in the souls. Shoot the enemy's orb before they do and you deny it: you steal those souls for yourself. If nobody touches it, it pops and goes to its owner.

So during the first ten minutes of every match, twelve people are shooting at tiny lights above dead soldiers. Try it. Green orbs are yours; orange orbs belong to the enemy hero down the street, who is also trying to shoot both.

Even at slow speed it's hard to catch everything, and that's the point. Laning in Deadlock is a constant, small contest of aim and attention: shoot troopers, watch for orbs, keep an eye on the enemy hero who is shooting at you while you shoot at lights. A good player quietly out-earns a careless one by thousands of souls in ten minutes, without ever winning a fight.

What souls buy you

Your total souls earned works like a level. Every few hundred souls you reach a new Boon, which raises your health and damage. At 600, 1,100 and 2,000 souls you unlock your three regular abilities, and at 3,800 your ultimate, the strongest one. The ladder ends at Boon 35, around 49,200 souls. Spending souls on items never lowers your level; the two run in parallel.

The second slider shows a small kindness in the rules. When several teammates stand near a trooper that dies, its souls are shared, but the shares add up to more than the original. Two heroes in a lane each get 54 instead of 50. Sharing a lane costs a little, not half.

Deadlock also leans hard against runaway leads, a problem other games call snowballing. Defeating an enemy who is far richer than their teammates pays a large bonus. Teams that fall behind earn up to 30% more from troopers and monsters. Souls from the monsters between lanes are "haunted" and can be lost if you die carrying them. Valve keeps adjusting these comeback rules; it weakened some in September 2026 and strengthened others a month later.

IV
Who you play, and what you buy

Forty-four heroes, a shop, and a hundred ways to build

Before a match starts, each of the twelve players picks a hero. In November 2026 there are 44 of them, and they are a strange crowd: a gargoyle, a jetpack salesman, a vampire who wants to run New York, a man and his giant armadillo, a chess master from Central Park.

Every hero has a gun with its own feel, a quick and a heavy melee attack, and four abilities: three regular ones and an ultimate, the big one. You don't start with them. They unlock as you collect souls, at 600, 1,100 and 2,000 for the regular three and 3,800 for the ultimate. Each ability can then be upgraded three times, and the upgrades often change what it does rather than just making it bigger.

Many team games assign fixed jobs: a tank, a healer, a damage dealer. Deadlock doesn't. Valve gives each hero three short descriptive tags instead, some practical ("Initiator," "Area Denial") and some just jokes ("Frogs," "zZzzZ"). Use them below to sort the cast. Tap or point at any hero to read who they are.

The dates tell their own story. Since 2025 heroes have arrived in batches of six with each big update, and players vote on the order they're released. One of the newest, the Rat King, started as a joke on the game's forums. Before that, Valve tested raw ideas in a separate mode called Hero Labs, where new heroes appeared with placeholder models and names so Valve could judge how they played before spending months on how they looked. A few never made it out.

The shop

Souls you haven't spent can be traded for items at shops around the map: one in each base, one beside each lane's first Guardian (it closes when that Guardian falls), and two hidden underground that both teams can use. There are about 156 items, sorted into three colored trees:

Orange

Weapon

Your gun: more damage, faster fire, bigger magazines, special effects when bullets land.

Green

Vitality

Staying alive: health, healing, resistance to damage, speed.

Purple

Spirit

Your abilities: stronger effects, shorter waits between uses, longer reach.

Every item has one of four prices: 800, 1,600, 3,200 or 6,400 souls. You have nine slots to fill. Three more open up during the match, one for your whole team each time you destroy an enemy Walker. That's the toy below.

The same hero can be built into three very different players. Pour souls into orange and your hero becomes a gunslinger who wins by aiming. Pour them into green and they become very hard to kill. Pour them into purple and their abilities take over. Buy a few items and watch the dot move across the triangle; the dotted line is the path your choices took.

Prices and slot counts are the game's. Most item names here are placeholders for whole tiers; the named ones (Extended Magazine, Stalker, Headhunter, Extra Health, Extra Spirit, Refresher) are real. Two real rules are built in. Sell something while you're still standing in the shop and you get every soul back, which forgives a misclick. Walk away first and you get half.

A hidden rule pushes players toward committing. Souls spent in one color also add a bonus to that color's core stat; the community wiki reports that 4,800 souls of Weapon items adds about 46% to weapon damage. Hybrids are allowed, but specialists get a discount on power. That tension, between what your hero is good at and what your team needs this particular match, is most of what experienced players mean when they talk about a "build."

V
Getting around

Movement: a city you can climb

In older games of this kind, the map is flat. You see it from above and your hero walks across it like a chess piece. Deadlock's city has rooftops, fire escapes, alleys, sewers and subway tunnels, and getting through it quickly is a skill of its own.

Every hero can run, jump, dash, slide and kick off walls. The fancy moves draw from a small budget called stamina. Most heroes have three bars of it, and one bar refills about every four and a half seconds. Spend it all and you're stuck walking until it comes back.

Below is a slice of the Cursed Apple: a brownstone stoop, two fire escapes, a gap between rooftops, and a tall building with a water tower on top. Get to the water tower. There is more than one way up. If you'd rather watch first, press Watch a route and the little figure in the fedora will show you one.

A few things in that little scene are true to the game. The pips over your head are the whole budget: every double jump or dash takes one, and you can watch them refill. The gap between the rooftops is too wide for a plain jump, even with a double jump, but a dash-jump clears it easily. The tall building needs wall jumps, and the free first one is a gift you'll want to save for exactly that moment. If you fall, nothing happens. Deadlock has no fall damage, so a drop from a roof is just a shortcut to the street.

Run without stopping for five seconds and your hero breaks into a sprint. In a real match, sprinting only happens when you haven't been in a fight recently, so it's how you cross the city between battles.

Ziplines: the lanes are also railways

Above each lane hangs a glowing rail in that lane's color: yellow for York, blue for Broadway, green for Greenwich. Heroes leave their base by riding it, hanging from a hook, at about 18 meters per second on Broadway and 21 on the side lanes, far faster than anyone can run.

Here is the clever part. Your zipline only reaches as far as your troopers have pushed. Win the tug of war from Chapter II and your rail stretches deeper into the enemy's half of the city, so your team gets back to the fight sooner after every trip to the shop or every death. Lose it and you walk.

Two upgrades make the rail even faster. Once a match reaches six minutes, each hero can trigger a speed boost of 80% for 32 seconds, usable once every six minutes. And when your team destroys the enemy's Base Guardians in a lane, your rail there becomes a permanent Express Line, also 80% faster. The slider has to reach the enemy base before that second button unlocks.

A zipline is not a safe place, though. If an enemy hero shoots you while you're riding, you're knocked off, take damage and land stunned, unless you've already been on the rail for three seconds.

Underground

Teleporters

Two linked pairs of booths sit in the subway tunnels beside the secret shops. Step in, wait four seconds, and you come out on the other side of the map.

Hero powers

Moves of their own

Many heroes bend these rules. Lash swings on a grapple, Ivy carries a teammate through the air, Mirage teleports across the city, and Mo & Krill burrow underground.

Up and down

Fights on many floors

A fight can start on a rooftop, spill down a fire escape and end in a sewer. Top-down games can't do that; in Deadlock, where you stand vertically matters as much as where you stand on the map.

VI
Thirty-five minutes

A match, minute by minute

Now you know the pieces: lanes, troopers, towers, souls, heroes, items. A match is those pieces switched on in a set order. The city wakes up on a schedule, and every few minutes something new appears that is worth fighting over.

Drag along the timeline, or press play. The minimap on the left shows the city with the Hidden King at the bottom and the Archmother at the top; filled shapes are standing towers. The times when things appear and when towers lose their armor come from the game's rules. Which towers fall, and when, is one made-up but typical match.

The middle rows hold the quiet machinery that makes matches end. The first towers start with heavy armor so nobody can rush them, and lose it on a fixed clock. By minute twelve they take extra damage; by eighteen, so do the Walkers. Waves come faster after twenty minutes and faster again after thirty-five. If you remember the AI Director from Valve's Left 4 Dead (Chapter VIII), this is the same idea built into a map: calm stretches, rising pressure, set-piece peaks.

Notice too how many objectives sit between the lanes. The Urn, the Rift, the monster camps and the Mid Boss all pull heroes away from their troopers. Every minute spent chasing one is a minute your lane is left to the stalemate you saw in Chapter II. That trade, between the lane and everything else, is most of what Deadlock players argue about.

What a newcomer actually sees

For the first ten minutes: two people on each team shooting soldiers, glowing orbs, and each other on a single street. Then, gradually, everyone starts moving: across rooftops, through sewers, along ziplines, converging on whatever just appeared. The last ten minutes are big twelve-person fights that end with one team walking into the other's base. An average match lasts about thirty-five minutes; long ones pass forty-five.

VII Where it comes from

A family tree written by fans

Deadlock looks new, but almost every idea in it has a parent, and many of those parents were made for free by hobbyists in their bedrooms.

Two families of games meet in Deadlock. The first is the strategy game, where you look down on a battlefield and command an army. The second is the team shooter, where you see the world through the eyes of one person holding a gun, and your team wins by working together. For about twenty-five years these families mostly lived apart. Deadlock is the moment they finally merge.

The strategy side changed shape because players kept modifying the games they loved. Blizzard's StarCraft (1998) came with a map editor, a tool that let anyone build new scenarios. Some fan, often credited as "Aeon64" (the author and date are still argued over), built a map called Aeon of Strife where each player controlled one strong hero instead of a whole army, and computer soldiers marched down three roads toward the enemy base. That small change is the seed of the whole genre.

Four years later, Warcraft III shipped with an even better editor, and a modder called Eul rebuilt the idea as Defense of the Ancients, or DotA. Others took it over and kept improving it, until an anonymous designer known only as IceFrog spent years tuning it. DotA spread through internet cafés across Asia and Europe. Companies noticed. Riot Games made League of Legends, and Valve hired IceFrog himself to make Dota 2.

Trace any line below and you can see this pattern repeat. Hollow rings are games made by fans. The small brass dots mark people Valve later hired.

The attempts to merge

People tried to blend the two families many times. Monday Night Combat (2010) had lanes, marching robots and turrets, played as a shooter. Smite (2014) moved the camera down behind your hero and made you aim. Epic's Paragon (2016) looked spectacular. Battleborn and Gigantic arrived soon after. Most of them closed within a few years. Follow those branches above and you'll see them end in a small tick mark.

Valve had something none of them did: the people behind both parents. The Team Fortress designers it hired in 1998 and the DotA designer it hired in 2009 were already under one roof. Deadlock grew quietly inside Valve from around 2018, under codenames like "Citadel" and "Neon Prime", before it was revealed in August 2024.

It is still officially a playtest. You can only play if a friend invites you, and Valve has never sold a copy. Even so, more people have played it at the same moment than ever played Team Fortress 2 at once.

Counting players is messy. Steam publishes how many people are playing at one moment; League of Legends and the phone MOBAs report monthly or daily players instead. Those numbers can't be put on the same chart, which is why League (over 100 million monthly players in 2016) and Honor of Kings (over 100 million daily players in 2020) appear here only in words.

VIII
The makers

Who is Valve, and why do people trust it?

Valve is a small private company in Bellevue, Washington, a few hundred people who make games and run Steam, the biggest store for PC games. Deadlock is their newest game. To understand why players pay such close attention to it, it helps to know how Valve works.

Gabe Newell and Mike Harrington left Microsoft and founded Valve in 1996. Their first game, Half-Life (1998), told its whole story while you played. The camera never cut away to a movie. A scientist got dragged into a vent in front of you; a guard opened a door for you. It felt like being inside a story rather than watching one.

In 2003 Valve launched Steam, first just to update its own games. Half-Life 2 required it, which put it on millions of computers, and other companies' games followed. Today Steam is the App Store of PC games, and it pays Valve's bills. That changes how Valve makes games: nothing has to ship on a deadline to keep the lights on. Estimates put Valve at roughly 350 to 385 employees in 2025–26. A single blockbuster game elsewhere can involve more than a thousand people.

A pattern runs through that list. Valve doesn't have a house genre. What it has is a set of habits, and three of them show up in every game they make. You can try each one below.

Habit one: make things readable at a glance

In a team game you have a fraction of a second to decide whether the figure across the street is a threat. Team Fortress 2 solved this with shape. Valve's 2007 paper on the game's art, "Illustrative Rendering in Team Fortress 2," describes reading order: first team (colour), then character (silhouette), then weapon (detail). The art borrowed from early-20th-century illustrators like J.C. Leyendecker and Norman Rockwell. It was a style chosen because it carries information.

Deadlock inherits this. Its heroes are a cast of strong, strange outlines: a gargoyle, a gangster with a tommy gun, a grandmother who travels with pigeons. Try it yourself. Start with the slider on the left, where all six figures are nearly the same soldier, and answer a few questions. Then drag the slider right.

When the shapes are alike you're guessing. When they differ, you know the healer by her lantern and coat before you can see a face. Nothing about the figures changed except their outline.

Habit two: design feelings over time

Left 4 Dead (2008) is a game about four survivors crossing a city full of zombies. Instead of placing every monster by hand, Valve built the AI Director, a system that estimates how stressed the players are and decides when to send a horde, when to back off, and when to let them breathe. Its designer, Michael Booth, described the goal as "structured unpredictability." The rhythm is build up, peak, relax, the same shape a horror film editor cuts toward.

Below, four survivors (white) hold a street. Compare a spawner that sends enemies at random with one that watches the stress line and paces itself. Watch the readout: random spawning leaves players either bored or swamped. You can also overrule both with a horde of your own.

Deadlock has its own version of this rhythm built into the map. Waves of soldiers arrive on a timer, big rewards appear at set minutes, and quiet stretches of farming alternate with sudden fights. You'll see the whole match laid out in Chapter VI.

Habit three: teach by letting people discover

Valve's games rarely explain themselves with text. Portal is built from "test chambers," and each one introduces exactly one idea, lets you try it safely, then combines it with ideas you already know. When playtesters got stuck, the team split the puzzle into smaller steps instead of adding instructions. Half-Life 2 hands you the Gravity Gun in a quiet yard; one chapter later you're in a town full of saw blades and you figure out the rest yourself.

Here are three tiny rooms with no words in them. Move with the arrow keys, or the buttons, or by tapping beside the white figure. Get to the door on the right.

Room one teaches that a button opens a door. Room two breaks that rule (this button only works while something sits on it) and offers the fix. Room three asks you to use both ideas twice. The red squares are a tiny version of what Valve's playtest team does at scale: watch where people hesitate, then change the room, not the person.

The habit behind the habits: watch people play

Valve's playtesting lead, the experimental psychologist Mike Ambinder, summed up the method in a 2009 talk:

"Game designs are hypotheses. Playtests are experiments."Mike Ambinder, Valve, GDC 2009

Valve brings in outsiders, watches silently, and measures what they do rather than what they say. It has used heatmaps of where players die, and even sweat and eye-tracking sensors. The same spirit explains its hiring: Team Fortress, Counter-Strike, Portal, Left 4 Dead and Dota 2 all began with outsiders who made something brilliant, and Valve hired them.

How Deadlock carries all of this

  • The playtest is the game. Deadlock has been "invite-only" since 2024. You get in when a friend invites you. Even so, it hit 303,347 players at once in October 2026, more than Team Fortress 2's all-time record, without ever going on sale.
  • Unfinished on purpose. New heroes sometimes arrive in an experimental mode with placeholder art, so Valve can test how they play before paying for how they look. Players have even voted on which heroes ship next.
  • Constant tuning. Patch notes are long, dry lists of small number changes across many heroes at once, in the style IceFrog used for Dota. In 2025 Valve switched from updates every two weeks to fewer, larger ones.
  • "Valve time." Development reportedly began around 2018 under the codename Citadel. Valve still calls the game unfinished.

Valve's habits don't guarantee a hit. Artifact launched with a price tag, heavy hype and little public testing, and it collapsed within months. Deadlock is the opposite experiment: years in public, shaped by the people playing it.

IX The setting

The Cursed Apple

Every Deadlock match happens in the same city. Its name is the Cursed Apple, a play on New York's old nickname, the Big Apple. The game's own files call the map dl_midtown.

It is New York with a hex on it. The skyline is real Manhattan, building for building in places. The streets are named after real streets. Then the occult arrives: ritual circles painted on rooftops, a newspaper that prints prophecy, a statue of Atlas holding a glowing orb in a fog-filled plaza, a boss monster living in the sewer under Broadway.

People argue about which decade it is. The towers belong to 1899 through 1932. The clothes look like the 1930s. After a big update in September 2026, the middle of the map turned into a neon Times Square that one critic called 1950s. The fair answer is that it is a collage of several decades of New York, all standing at once. This chapter takes the city apart and puts real New York next to each piece.

From a street grid to three lanes

In 1811, New York's planners drew a grid over the whole island of Manhattan: twelve numbered avenues running up its length, and 155 cross streets. The grid is tilted about 29 degrees east of true north, because it follows the island instead of the compass. Below Houston Street the old town was already built, so its crooked streets survived.

One road ignored the grid. Broadway was there first, and it cuts diagonally across the avenues. Everywhere it crosses one, the city got a square: Union Square, Madison Square (home of the triangular Flatiron Building), Herald Square, Times Square.

A Deadlock map needs lanes: long roads from one team's home to the other's, where the computer-controlled soldiers march. Valve built them out of Manhattan's long streets. The three lanes are named York, Broadway and Greenwich, after York Avenue, Broadway and Greenwich Street. Until February 25, 2025 there was a fourth lane, named Park, after Park Avenue. Valve removed it to make the map tighter.

The two teams live at opposite ends, like the two ends of the island. Each team is backed by an occult patron. The Hidden King lives downtown in a run-down factory full of speakers. The Archmother lives uptown in a marble museum, and she wears a cracked mask that looks a lot like the Statue of Liberty's face. Uptown money against downtown labour is a very New York argument. Early in development, the two bases were named after the city's two great rival railroad stations, Grand Central and Penn Station.

Why the towers are shaped like wedding cakes

Look at the skyline in Deadlock and you'll see towers that narrow in steps as they rise. That shape is a law. In 1915 the Equitable Building went up on Broadway as one enormous block, and it threw its neighbours into shadow. The next year, New York passed the first comprehensive zoning code in the United States.

The 1916 rule worked like this. A building could rise straight up from the sidewalk to a height set by the width of the street. Above that, it had to stay under a slanted line drawn up from the middle of the street, so light could still reach the pavement. A tower covering no more than a quarter of the lot could rise as high as its owner liked.

An architectural illustrator named Hugh Ferriss drew what this law allowed, in four famous stages, in 1922. His moody charcoal skylines, collected in The Metropolis of Tomorrow (1929), are the look of every noir and occult New York since, Deadlock's included. Try the four stages yourself.

A note on roofs. A 2026 GameSpot essay credits the 1916 law for the city's copper mansard roofs, the steep French-style roofs on hotels like the Plaza. Those roofs are older. They come from the Second Empire and Beaux-Arts styles of roughly 1865 to 1910. The 1916 law gave New York its setbacks.

The skyline is a real race

In December 2024, a Steam user named silvershroud published a guide called "Real New York buildings in Deadlock, explained." It puts thirty real buildings and street details beside screenshots from the game, in date order from St. Paul's Chapel (1766) to the Cities Service Building (1932), each with its architect, year and style. Most of the matches are towers on the horizon: you see them from rooftops, but you can't walk into them.

Put those towers in date order and they tell the story of the great height race. Between 1899 and 1931, the title of world's tallest building changed hands again and again in Lower Manhattan and Midtown. Below, the four elevated railways that once ran above the avenues come down one by one.

Trains in the sky

From 1868, New Yorkers rode steel elevated railways above the avenues: the Ninth, Sixth, Third and Second Avenue lines. The city tore them down between 1938 and the mid-1950s. Deadlock has its own rails over the streets. Every lane has a zip-line strung above it, and a player can hook on and ride toward the fighting. A GameSpot writer described the game's "floating railways" cutting through the sky. Reading the zip-lines as the El's ghost is our own interpretation, but it fits.

Where this came from

Game rules and numbers are from the community-maintained Deadlock Wiki as of November 2026, cross-checked against Valve's update notes and press coverage. Deadlock is unfinished and changes often, so specific numbers will drift. The simulations simplify on purpose: heroes have one gun, waves are uniform, and items are left out.

  • Deadlock Wiki: Map, Troopers, Souls, Soul orb, Lane Guardian, Walker, Patron, Backdoor Protection, Items, Heroes, Movement, Zipline, Mid Boss, Development.
  • Valve, Deadlock news on Steam.
  • PC Gamer, four lanes to three (Feb 2025); Deadlock passes TF2's player record (Oct 2026).
  • KeenGamer, 303,347 concurrent players while invite-only (Oct 2026).
  • Mike Ambinder, Valve's Approach to Playtesting, GDC 2009.
  • Mitchell, Francke & Eng, Illustrative Rendering in Team Fortress 2, NPAR 2007.
